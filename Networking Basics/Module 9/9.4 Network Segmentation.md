
## **Ethernet Broadcasts & Broadcast Domains 

Ethernet LANs use broadcasts to communicate with all devices in the same local network. Some protocols rely on broadcast messages to find information such as MAC addresses or DHCP servers.

---

### **What Is an Ethernet Broadcast?**

- A frame sent to **all devices** on the same LAN segment.
    
- Used when a device does not know specific destination information (like MAC address).
    
- Switches forward broadcasts out **all ports except the incoming port**.
    

---

### **Examples of Ethernet Broadcasts**

#### **1. ARP Request**

- Sent when a device needs to learn the MAC address of a known IPv4 address.
    
- The ARP request is broadcast to all hosts in the LAN.
    
- Switches flood it; routers block it.

![ARP Request](/Imagesn/83.png)

#### **2. DHCP Discover**

- Sent by a host that needs an IPv4 address.
    
- Broadcasts to find a DHCP server.
    
- Switches flood it; routers block it unless a DHCP relay is configured.
    
![ARP Request](/Imagesn/84.png)

---

### **Broadcast Behavior**

- **Switches**
    
    - Forward (flood) broadcasts out every interface except the one that received it.
        
    - Do _not_ stop broadcasts within the same LAN.
        
- **Routers**
    
    - **Do not forward broadcasts**.
        
    - Block broadcasts from crossing into other networks.
        

---

### **Layer 2 Broadcast Domains**

- A broadcast domain is the **area where a Layer 2 broadcast can reach**.
    
- Switches keep devices in the same broadcast domain.
    
- Routers **separate** or **segment** broadcast domains.
    
![ARP Request](/Imagesn/85.png)

---

>[!notes]- Key Points
>
>- Broadcasts stay within a single Layer 2 broadcast domain.
  >  
>- Routers prevent broadcasts from crossing into other networks.
   > 
>- ARP and DHCP rely on Ethernet broadcasts inside a LAN.

## **Broadcast Domains and Segmentation 

Ethernet networks rely on broadcasts for essential functions like ARP and DHCP. However, broadcasts can create unnecessary traffic if they reach devices that don’t need the information. Routers help manage and limit this by dividing networks into multiple broadcast domains.

---

### **What Are Broadcasts in a LAN?**

- A broadcast is a message sent to **all devices** in a local Ethernet network.
    
- Used by:
    
    - **ARP** → To find the MAC address of a known IPv4 address.
        
    - **DHCP Discover** → To find a DHCP server when getting an IP address.
        

---

### **Switch Behavior (Layer 2)**

- Switches **flood broadcasts** out all ports except the incoming one.
    
- They **do NOT stop broadcasts**.
    
- Therefore, all devices connected to the same switch (or chain of switches) are part of **one broadcast domain**.
    

---

### **Router Behavior (Layer 3)**

- **Routers DO NOT forward broadcasts.**
    
- If a router receives a broadcast on one interface, it **drops** it and does NOT send it to any other interface.
    
- **Each router interface creates a separate broadcast domain.**
    

---

### **Broadcast Domains**

- A broadcast domain is the area within which a broadcast packet can travel.
    
- **Switches** keep devices in the same broadcast domain.
    
- **Routers** segment and isolate broadcast domains.
    

---

>[!tip]
>
>- ARP and DHCP depend on broadcast communication within a LAN.
>    
>- Too many broadcasts can cause unnecessary traffic, which is why segmentation is important.
   > 
>- Routers naturally limit broadcast spread by dividing networks into separate broadcast domains.

## Broadcast Domains and Segmentation 

Broadcasts can quickly reach every device on a network segment, but often only a subset of those devices need the information.  
In Ethernet LANs, broadcasts are used for ARP, DHCP, and other services, while routers deliberately stop broadcast traffic, creating isolated broadcast domains.

___
## What is a Broadcast Domain?

- A logical area of a network in which all nodes can receive broadcast frames sent on the same link layer segment.
- Broadcasts are confined to the _subnet_  that they originate from.

### How Routers Segment Broadcast Domains

- A router’s interface is a _boundary_ that separates one broadcast domain from another.
- When a router receives a broadcast on interface Gi0/0, it _does not_ forward that broadcast to its other interfaces (e.g., Gi0/1).
- **Result:** Each router interface connects to a distinct broadcast domain, preventing network‑wide flooding.
![Router Segments the Broadcast Domains](/Imagesn/86.png)

### Broadcast Usage in Ethernet LANs

- ARP: Layer‑2 broadcast to map an IPv4 address → MAC address.
- DHCP: Client broadcasts to locate a DHCP server for IP configuration.
- Switches: Forward broadcasts out _all_ ports except the one that received the frame, extending the broadcast across connected switches and devices _within the same VLAN_.

>[!Tip]
>
>- Routers = Broadcast barriers since they stop broadcast propagation, creating isolated domains.
>- Switches = Broadcast facilitators since they propagate broadcasts across VLAN‑connected ports.
>- Proper segmentation reduces broadcast traffic, improves security, and limits the impact of broadcast storms.
